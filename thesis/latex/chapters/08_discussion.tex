\section{Interpretation Strategy}

The final interpretation treats the verified simulation evidence as one campaign while selecting valid comparison blocks for each claim. The matched 60/120-satellite block supports California--India event-window statements. The India 180-satellite extension uses the same 773-task contract, so its balanced CN30--CN100 cases can be paired with the corresponding 120-satellite block. This creates a controlled India scale comparison without implying that an unexecuted California 180-satellite population exists.

The interpretation is therefore conditional but complete. The available evidence is sufficient to answer the research questions within the declared scope. The report identifies which statements are supported by matched regional evidence, which apply to the India large-fleet extension, and which require a new experiment.

\section{Method Contribution}

The communication framework was successfully adapted to a multi-task wildfire use case. Remote-sensing detections were converted into requests carrying location, release time, priority, deadline, and packet size. The simulator enforced the sequence release, reception, and post-reception access before awarding a follow-up observation. This prevents geometric opportunity from being counted when the relevant spacecraft learns about a task too late.

The contribution is a communication-constrained follow-up and dissemination workflow, not a complete wildfire-response chain or a satellite scheduling optimizer. The model begins after creation of a thermal-event task and ends at a modeled follow-up opportunity. Instrument pointing, cloud and smoke screening, image production, downlink of imagery, alert distribution, and human response remain outside the evidence. This boundary aligns the study identity with the implemented mechanism.

\section{Workload Context}

The matched regional block shows that the event-window workloads change both values and architecture order. India has higher observation fulfilment but weaker complete dissemination, lower useful coverage, substantially later P100 $T_{100}$, and lower robustness retention. Observation can remain high when one informed satellite later reaches the target even though full dissemination to all useful recipients is incomplete. The larger India task population also increases absolute traffic and contention.

Priority-stratified results show why pooled latency alone would be misleading. Deadlines and packet sizes are functions of priority, so the censoring process is not independent of task class. Direct deadline attainment, class-specific Kaplan--Meier summaries, and RMST with fixed class horizons therefore form the defensible timing result. Equal-weight class summaries are used when a cross-window aggregate is required.

Here, ``California'' and ``India'' are shorthand for two selected event windows. They are not random samples from either region. Matching keeps the spacecraft architecture fixed, but it cannot control season, event density, spatial pattern, task composition, or priority mix. The difference is therefore interpreted as a contrast between two workload cases, not as a population-level California--India effect.

\section{RQ1: Central Node Fraction and Forwarding Policy}

RQ1 does not have one Central Node curve; it has different curves for different forwarding rules. In the matched regional block, bounded observation improves at low and intermediate fractions and then falls. The 180-satellite India block shows the same interaction more clearly: bounded observation drops from 88.15\% at CN30 to 43.52\% at CN100, whereas the unlimited diagnostic improves in observation, strict completion, coverage, and timing toward CN100. High participation is useful when forwarding is permissive, but can be harmful when the copy and hop limits act on the full package.

The experiment changes four things together when the Central Node label is applied: link eligibility, forwarding preference, deterministic placement, and link budget. The high-CN decline can therefore be attributed only to the implemented package. Congestion or routing concentration may explain the pattern, but the present outputs cannot distinguish that explanation from the other package components. Component ablations and detailed forwarding traces would be required before assigning a cause to ``centralization'' itself.

At 180 satellites, peer and hybrid routing each improve strict completion by about 0.94 percentage points relative to Central Node-only routing. They also add roughly 29,300 Mbit per case. The improvement is measurable, but small compared with the added communication volume in this experiment.

\section{RQ2: Sensitivity to Workload, Packet Size, Geometry, and Failures}

The sensitivity results change both the metric values and the order of architectures. On the matched 60/120-satellite grid, the India workload has higher observation fulfilment than California, but complete dissemination is weaker and later. Packet size produces one of the largest changes. Fourfold packets lower strict completion by 37.02 points in the matched India block and by 33.67 points in the 180-satellite extension. In that extension, they also add about 133,628 Mbit per case.

No constellation factor has one monotonic effect that can be separated from the rest of the grid. Satellite count, plane count, altitude, and inclination all change the temporal contact opportunities, and their effects differ with Central Node fraction and event window. The marginal plots average over those other factors. They show how the tested grid behaves, not an isolated causal coefficient. Geometry clearly changes the feasible routes and the ranking, but the preferred level still depends on the remaining design choices.

Failures also alter the ranking. Across the final verified campaign, ground-station outage is the most damaging single family tested. This identifies the one-station Munich baseline as an important vulnerability, but it does not determine how many replacement stations are needed or where they should be located.

Combined load and failure are also important: the worst 180-satellite cell retains only 57.49\% of its baseline strict completion. RQ2 is therefore answered for the workloads, packet multipliers, geometries, and disturbances that were executed. Observation radius, alternative placement, task construction, and package ablations remain outside the evidence.

\section{RQ3: Architecture Trade-Offs and Decision Stability}

No design dominates all objectives. The matched regional block yields large event-window Pareto sets and a five-case cross-window core. The 180-satellite extension contains 61 exact Pareto cases among 216 balanced candidates, and its highest rank-1 acceptability is only 26.55\%. These results show that the preferred design depends on how mission completion, observation, timing, fleet burden, Central Node count, traffic, and robustness are valued.

The pairwise fronts make that dependence concrete. In the balanced 180-satellite block, completion versus traffic retains 10 non-dominated cases and observation versus traffic retains 6, while completion versus complete-dissemination time retains only 1. These smaller fronts are useful for explaining a single trade, but none replaces the 61-case frontier computed from the full objective vector. A point can be dominated in one two-objective view and still remain efficient when the other mission and resource objectives are restored.

The final architecture product is a tiered portfolio. Its five-case core is supported in both event windows, while the 180-satellite candidates extend the portfolio for the India workload. Within that large-fleet tier, CN40/CN50 can offer lower traffic, whereas CN90/CN100 provides faster complete dissemination at substantially higher communication burden. The tiers are reported together, with the evidence scope stated for each candidate.

The preferred Central Node fraction moves when the forwarding policy, task size, failure family, event window, or objective weights change. Random family-weight draws produce different winners across the campaign. This is interpreted as sensitivity of the decision, not as stakeholder agreement. The reference coefficients are engineering choices made for the analysis. The \SI{700}{\kilo\metre} access radius, priority rules, deadlines, packet sizes, and clustering settings are likewise fixed assumptions.

\section{Importance, Non-Significance, and Engineering Meaning}

Three findings are large enough to change an architecture choice.

First, routing bounds reverse the high-CN trend: the bounded package becomes worse while the unlimited diagnostic becomes better. Second, larger packets cause double-digit losses in strict completion and large traffic increases. Message size is therefore a design variable, not a software detail. Third, ground-station outage causes the largest individual robustness loss tested across the final verified campaign. For the implemented baseline, the single station is a more immediate weakness than randomly removing a comparable share of spacecraft.

The likelihood--consequence matrix adds prioritization without disguising its assumptions. Ground-station and link-window outage are High screening items under the declared likelihood categories; the remaining families are Moderate or Low. The consequence axis is measured, whereas the likelihood axis is not. A different reliability assessment can therefore move a disturbance vertically and change its class. The matrix supports the order of follow-up engineering work, but the measured loss and its across-architecture standard deviation remain the primary simulation evidence.

Several smaller differences need restraint. Peer and hybrid routing are numerically almost identical at 180 satellites, so the two labels are not treated as evidence of separate mechanisms. The roughly one-point peer-routing gain in strict completion comes with another 29,300~Mbit per case.

Altitude has a weaker pooled association than inclination, plane count, or Central Node fraction. This does not make altitude irrelevant, because interactions and the tested 500--1000~km range can hide a conditional effect. Similarly, the near-perfect correlation between traffic and event count is an accounting redundancy, not a new physical result.

The reported intervals have a deliberately limited interpretation. They describe the stability of a mean across the enumerated base architectures or seeded disturbance trials. They are not confidence intervals for all fires, all constellations, or either geographic region. Changes are therefore judged against mission thresholds and resource burden rather than using the interval as a test of a geographic-population null hypothesis.

\section{Interpretation of the 180-Satellite Campaign Extension}

The India 180-satellite extension has 282 verified cases. Each uses all 773 intended tasks and contains the expected 539 scenario identifiers. The task, normalized-task, case-allow-list, and run-contract hashes agree with the verifier. Aggregate curves and rankings use only CN30--CN100, where all 27 base architectures are present. This avoids giving unequal weight to the partially completed low-CN levels.

The balanced block gives a direct India 120-to-180 comparison for 216 architectures under unlimited routing. The 180-satellite fleet adds 1.23 points of observation, 3.99 points of strict completion, and 1.55 points of useful coverage. P100 $T_{100}$ is 44.68 minutes shorter, but traffic is 25,827 Mbit per case higher. This is a performance--traffic trade, not unconditional dominance by the larger fleet. It also says nothing about a California--India comparison at 180 satellites, and CN0--CN20 is excluded from balanced aggregate claims.

The task inconsistency exposed a gap in the original restart checks. Scenario identifiers alone were not enough: the run also had to be tied to input hashes, regional and priority counts, the case allow-list, and a reset positional index. A success marker proves that a programmed job ended, but not that old and new partitions used identical scientific inputs. The verified package performs these checks and is the only source used for the final 180-satellite results.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\subsection{Construct validity}

Observation is a geometric \SI{700}{\kilo\metre} access proxy rather than a sensor-quality or imaging-feasibility model. Packet sizes describe task messages, not imagery. The Central Node role is a bundled communication treatment rather than a separately costed spacecraft class. P100 $T_{100}$ measures complete useful-recipient dissemination, not first awareness or end-to-end alert latency.

\subsection{Internal validity}

The factorial grid controls the declared Walker factors but not alternative task rules, node placements, or package components. High-CN aggregate patterns are associations under one implementation. The verified India population includes all intended 773 tasks, but the 180-satellite case selection remains incomplete at CN0--CN20.

\subsection{External validity}

External validity is limited to what was run. The evidence covers the listed constellations, routing rules, deadlines, link assumptions, one ground station, and disturbance families in two event windows. The 180-satellite findings apply to the 773-task India window. A larger fleet, a California 180-satellite layer, or another event window would be a new experiment rather than an extension of these numbers.

\subsection{Statistical conclusion validity}

The base-clustered intervals refer to the enumerated architecture blocks, not to a geographic population. Thirty failure replicates and ten combined-stress replicates are sufficient for the mean-response analysis used here, but not for a fine low-tail ranking without convergence checks. Correlated metrics are screened before weighting, and exact Pareto membership is calculated before applying a compensatory score.

\section{Decision Implication}

The engineering output is one tiered portfolio. CN40/CN50 provides a lower-resource balance. CN90/CN100 gives stronger India completion but uses more traffic. The 120-satellite CN100 case is defensible only if its faster complete dissemination justifies the larger fleet and communication burden. The 180-satellite comparison adds the next scale step: better completion and timing than the matched 120-satellite cases at a cost of about 25.8~Gbit more task traffic per case. Its candidates are included in the same portfolio with an India-workload scope label.

Before an operational commitment, the mission owner should provide preference weights and feasibility thresholds, confirm the task-message product, and evaluate at least observation radius, placement, package components, and alternative ground networks on a small representative set. Those steps refine the decision; they are not evidence already contained in this thesis.
