\section{Work Developed in This Thesis}
This thesis develops a complete path from a wildfire detection to an architecture decision for a distributed satellite system. Sentinel-3 active-fire products are converted into time-stamped follow-up tasks; a temporal simulator then evaluates whether each task can be transmitted through the satellite network and received before a useful target access. The architecture study varies Walker geometry, fleet size, Central Node participation, forwarding policy, packet size, and component failures. The resulting outputs are checked for completeness and input consistency before mission performance, timing, traffic, robustness, Pareto efficiency, and decision sensitivity are calculated.

Figure~\ref{fig:mission_chain} locates the contribution within the operational chain. The implemented work covers task construction, satellite acquisition, inter-satellite dissemination, ground-station delivery, campaign execution, and post-processing. Central Nodes affect the dissemination stage by providing an additional relay role; they do not command the constellation and do not use different sensors.

\section{Motivation}
Coverage is necessary for a rapid-response Earth-observation mission, but it is not sufficient. A satellite can pass over a target within minutes and still miss the opportunity if the task arrives after the pass. The opposite can also happen: a request can spread through the network while no informed satellite obtains a useful access before the deadline. Information delivery and orbital access are therefore treated as two connected stages of one operational problem.

Wildfire follow-up makes this timing problem concrete. Each source product becomes a request with a position, release time, priority, deadline, and message size. The request must cross intermittent ground-to-space and space-to-space contacts, and a later access counts only if the observing satellite has already received the complete request. Failure can therefore occur through late communication or through the absence of a suitable post-reception access.

A Central Node is a satellite with an additional relay role. In the implemented model, that role changes link eligibility, the order in which forwarding candidates are considered, and the communication range assumed for a link with a Central Node endpoint. Nothing else about the spacecraft changes: it has the same orbit, sensor, processor, and reliability model. This is close to the information-broker role used in federated satellite concepts \citep{Scrocciolani2024,Messina2025}, while observation authority remains distributed.

The relevant design question is not a yes-or-no question about Central Nodes. With only a few, useful temporal paths may still be missing. With many, the system may create extra traffic or depend too heavily on one forwarding mechanism. The answer may also change with geometry, workload, packet size, or routing limits. Performance and burden are therefore compared across the full Central Node fraction sweep rather than selecting a fraction from one metric.

\section{Problem Statement}
The adapted problem contains many tasks released at different times, four priority classes, finite packets, different deadlines, and two event windows with very different loads. The engineering problem is:

Determine how Walker-constellation geometry, routing policy, and the fraction of Central Nodes affect deadline-constrained task dissemination and post-reception wildfire observation, while retaining the communication burden and response to failures needed for an architecture decision.

Two uses of the word robustness are separated throughout the report. Physical robustness is the retained mission performance after a node, link, ground-station, or capacity disturbance. Decision stability is the persistence of an architecture ranking when the objective weights change. A design can be stable under a weighting model and still perform poorly after a physical failure.

\section{Aim and Objectives}
The aim is to construct a traceable performance-resource-robustness trade-space for communication-constrained wildfire follow-up in a distributed satellite system. Four objectives support that aim:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Convert Sentinel-3 active-fire detections into a reproducible task table with location, release time, priority, deadline, packet size, and source provenance.
\item Simulate reception, finite-capacity forwarding, and post-reception observation over a controlled Walker-constellation and Central Node design grid.
\item Compare mission performance, communication burden, and failure response without treating one metric or an unadjusted correlation as a complete architecture decision.
\item Identify Pareto-efficient and decision-stable candidates while stating the assumptions under which the shortlist is valid.
\end{enumerate}
Task preparation, simulation, and decision support remain separate objectives. This separation shows whether a changed conclusion originates in the task definition, the simulated architecture, or the preference model used for ranking.

\section{Research Questions}
The report answers three research questions:

\textbf{RQ1.} How do Central Node fraction and forwarding policy change timely task dissemination and follow-up observation in the implemented distributed satellite system?

\textbf{RQ2.} How do workload, packet size, constellation geometry, and injected failures change performance and the relative ranking of architectures?

\textbf{RQ3.} Which architectures provide defensible trade-offs among observation fulfilment, deadline-complete dissemination, communication burden, resource proxies, and robustness?

Task generation forms part of the experimental method rather than a research question in its own right. Observation-radius sensitivity, alternative placement rules, and individual Central Node components are not assigned numerical conclusions because dedicated experiments for those questions were not executed.

\section{Expected Relationships}
Three expectations guide the analysis. First, an initial set of preferred relays should open useful temporal paths, while later additions should offer less once most relevant paths are already available \citep{Burleigh2022,Araniti2015,CCSDS2019}. Second, a lower delay may require more transfers and more differentiated nodes; delay alone is therefore not enough for selection. Third, the preferred architecture should change with workload and with the value assigned to capacity, deadlines, and resource burden. These are expectations derived from the literature and model structure, not simulation findings.

\section{Scope and Boundaries}
The simulated mission begins when a processed wildfire task is released. It ends when task reception and a possible follow-up observation have been evaluated. Thermal measurement, onboard fire detection, cloud screening, image production, image downlink, and delivery of an alert to civil-protection authorities are outside the model.

The Central Node fraction is the primary treatment. Satellite count, plane count, altitude, and inclination form the architecture context. Packet-size multipliers, routing policy, node and link failures, ground-station outage, capacity degradation, and objective weights are tested sensitivities. The following assumptions remain fixed in the completed campaign:

\begin{itemize}
\item one deterministic balanced Central Node placement rule;
\item one bundled Central Node treatment combining eligibility, preference, and a link-budget multiplier;
\item a 700 km observation-radius proxy;
\item one Munich ground station in the baseline;
\item two selected event windows rather than a statistical sample of global fire conditions; and
\item engineering resource proxies rather than monetary lifecycle cost.
\end{itemize}

The final Fresh V2 campaign is analysed as one body of evidence. Its matched California and India blocks cover 60- and 120-satellite architectures using the intended 32-task and 773-task inputs. The campaign also contains 282 verified India cases at 180 satellites under the same 773-task contract. The complete CN30--CN100 subset supports a controlled India 120-to-180 comparison for 216 matched architectures. A California--India contrast at 180 satellites is not made because a corresponding California population was not executed. The earlier 741-task execution and its correction remain documented in Section~4.8 and Appendix~C as part of the provenance record, but none of its values enters the final results.

\section{Contributions}
The thesis makes three connected contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A traceable wildfire-task input.} The final table links Sentinel-3 product records to de-duplicated fire-region tasks and preserves the information needed to reproduce priority and deadline assignments.
\item \textbf{A communication-aware architecture campaign.} The model separates physical opportunities, accepted transfers, task reception, and observation. It evaluates the same task rows across a Walker and Central Node grid with finite capacity and deterministic replay.
\item \textbf{A robustness-aware decision method.} Raw metrics are validated before Pareto filtering. Failure response and objective-weight sensitivity are reported independently, leaving the final portfolio conditional and auditable.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report Structure}
Chapter 2 brings together distributed coordination, routing over scheduled contacts, and active-fire sensing. Chapter 3 documents the conversion from Sentinel-3 detections to the frozen task table, and Chapter 4 describes the simulation. Chapter 5 fixes the experimental and post-processing rules before the results are examined. Chapter 6 retains the historical nominal comparison, while Chapter 7 presents the completed Fresh V2 campaign across 60, 120, and 180 satellites. Chapters 8 and 9 interpret the combined evidence, answer the research questions, and state the conditional architecture portfolio. Operational commands, file formats, and checkpoint details are placed in the appendices and repository.
