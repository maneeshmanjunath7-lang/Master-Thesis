Rapid wildfire follow-up by a distributed satellite system requires more than geometric access to the affected area. A task produced after an initial detection must reach a suitable satellite before its next useful overflight, despite intermittent contacts and finite link capacity. Assigning selected spacecraft a Central Node relay role can create additional dissemination opportunities, but it can also increase traffic and interact unfavourably with bounded forwarding. This thesis investigates that architecture trade-off through an end-to-end workflow that connects remote-sensing detections, task dissemination, later target access, robustness testing, and multi-objective decision support.

Sentinel-3 SLSTR active-fire detections are converted into traceable tasks containing a location, release time, priority, deadline, and message size. A discrete-event model propagates each task through time-varying ground-to-space and inter-satellite contacts. A follow-up observation is counted only when the observing satellite has received the complete task before the relevant access. Central Nodes change relay eligibility, forwarding preference, and communication range, while orbit, payload, processing capability, and component reliability remain unchanged. The final verified simulation campaign evaluates Walker constellations with 60, 120, and 180 satellites across plane count, altitude, inclination, Central Node fraction, routing policy, packet size, and disturbance conditions. California and India are compared on matched 60- and 120-satellite architectures. A verified 282-case India block at 180 satellites provides 216 matched comparisons between the 120- and 180-satellite fleets.

Post-processing separates observation fulfilment from complete dissemination, useful-recipient coverage, completion time, communication traffic, and robustness before Pareto and rank-acceptability analysis. The matched regional comparison demonstrates that workload composition changes both metric values and architecture preference: the India workload achieves 4.66 percentage points higher observation fulfilment than California, but 2.98 points lower strict completion, 5.95 points lower useful-recipient coverage, and approximately 175 minutes longer worst-case dissemination. Quadrupling task size reduces strict completion by 14.94 points in California and 37.02 points in India. Within the matched India fleet comparison, increasing the constellation from 120 to 180 satellites improves observation fulfilment by 1.23 points and strict completion by 3.99 points and reduces worst-case dissemination time by 44.68 minutes, at the cost of 25,827 Mbit more task traffic per case. High Central Node fractions help under unlimited routing but can perform poorly when copy and hop limits are active. Ground-station outage is the strongest single disturbance, while larger packets combined with satellite failure produce the greatest compound loss.

The central finding is that geometric coverage, dissemination performance, and resource demand cannot be represented by one metric or one universally optimal architecture. Mission value depends on whether information arrives before the useful observation opportunity and on the traffic required to achieve that outcome. The resulting contribution is therefore not a single recommended constellation, but a traceable architecture portfolio that exposes the trade-offs among workload, timing, routing limits, communication burden, and robustness.
